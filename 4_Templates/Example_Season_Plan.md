
# Example Season Plan

This is a filled-out Season Plan for a fictional solo practitioner (a senior engineer transitioning to a tech lead role). Use this as reference when creating your own.

---

## Context
**Date**: January 2025
**Season**: Q1 2025 (13 weeks: Jan 6 – Apr 6)

I've been an individual contributor for 5 years and was recently asked to step into a tech lead role. The team is small (4 engineers), the codebase is mature but has technical debt, and we're preparing for a major feature launch in Q2. Personally, I'm excited but anxious—I've never led a team before, and I'm worried about losing my technical edge while learning leadership.

The company is stable, my personal life is relatively calm (no major transitions), and I have decent capacity. My main challenge is learning to balance technical work with people leadership.

---

## Season Narrative

**This season is about establishing myself as a technical leader while maintaining my craft.**

I'm learning to lead without abandoning what makes me effective as an engineer. This means: building trust with my team, clarifying our technical direction, and starting to think strategically rather than just tactically. I'm also committed to not burning out—I've seen too many new leads overcommit and crash.

By the end of this season, I want to feel grounded in my new role, have established key leadership practices, and have the team operating with more clarity and autonomy.

---

## Outcomes

### 1. Team operates with clear technical direction
The team knows what we're building toward, why it matters, and how decisions get made. We have a lightweight technical roadmap that guides our work.

**Success looks like**: A documented technical strategy, team can articulate our direction, fewer "what should we work on?" conversations.

### 2. Established sustainable leadership practices
I have regular rhythms for 1-on-1s, team meetings, and technical oversight. I'm not firefighting or doing everything myself.

**Success looks like**: Weekly 1-on-1s scheduled and happening, team retrospectives every 2 weeks, I have time to think strategically.

### 3. Reduced critical technical debt in authentication system
The auth system is brittle and blocks the Q2 feature launch. We need to stabilize it without a full rewrite.

**Success looks like**: Auth system has test coverage, major security gaps addressed, team confident we can build on it.

### 4. Personal leadership foundation is established
I understand my leadership approach, have support systems in place, and am learning publicly about what works and what doesn't.

**Success looks like**: Regular coaching sessions with my manager, clear personal practices (journaling, reflection), comfortable admitting what I don't know.

---

## Story Threads

| Thread | Description | Linked Outcomes |
|--------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Building team trust** | Creating psychological safety, being present in 1-on-1s, demonstrating follow-through | 2, 4 |
| **Developing strategic thinking** | Learning to think beyond this sprint, connecting technical work to business outcomes, saying no to low-impact work | 1, 4 |
| **Strengthening technical foundation** | Addressing debt systematically, establishing testing practices, improving code quality | 3 |
| **Growing leadership capacity** | Learning to delegate, giving effective feedback, holding boundaries | 2, 4 |
| **Maintaining technical craft** | Still writing code, staying sharp, modeling good engineering | 1, 3 |

---

## Boundaries

**What I will NOT do this season:**

1. **No weekend work except true emergencies** – I need to model sustainability for the team
2. **No taking on additional projects outside my team** – I'm learning one new thing (leadership), not five
3. **No redesigning the entire system** – We're stabilizing, not rebuilding
4. **No skipping 1-on-1s to "get work done"** – Leadership *is* the work now
5. **No saying yes to every request** – I'm learning to prioritize and decline

These boundaries protect my capacity and focus. They're the things I'll be tempted to do but shouldn't.

---

## How This Season Plan Guides Weekly Movements

Each week during my Weekly Review, I'll choose 3–5 movements aligned with these outcomes. For example:

**Week 1 movements** might include:
- "Establish 1-on-1 rhythm with all team members" (Outcome 2)
- "Draft initial technical roadmap for team feedback" (Outcome 1)
- "Begin auth system assessment and test coverage analysis" (Outcome 3)

**Week 6 movements** might include:
- "Facilitate team discussion on decision-making framework" (Outcome 1)
- "Complete security review of auth system with team" (Outcome 3)
- "Have coaching conversation with manager on delegation" (Outcome 4)

The Season Plan gives me direction. The weekly movements keep me moving.

---

## Mid-Season Check-In (Week 6–7)

Halfway through the season, I'll ask:
- Are these outcomes still true?
- What's working? What's not?
- What needs to shift?
- Am I honoring my boundaries?

The Season Plan can evolve. It's a guide, not a cage.

---

## Notes for Practitioners

### Why this Season Plan works:
1. **It's grounded in reality**: The context section names what's actually happening
2. **The narrative tells a story**: Not just a list of goals—a coherent season arc
3. **Outcomes are meaningful**: Each one matters and connects to the narrative
4. **Story Threads create connection**: They show how different outcomes relate
5. **Boundaries are specific and protective**: They name real temptations

### Common mistakes this avoids:
- ❌ Too many outcomes (4 is manageable given context)
- ❌ Vague aspirations ("be a better leader" → specific outcomes instead)
- ❌ No boundaries (this person knows their tendency to overcommit)
- ❌ Ignoring context (the Season Plan acknowledges the transition)
- ❌ Rigid planning (note the mid-season check-in)
